Setting Up Google Analytics: How We Really Do It

31 July 2026 4 min read Sem van Wijk

"Add Analytics" sounds like a five-minute checkbox: paste a code into the site, done. For us it takes a bit longer — and for good reason, because the standard approach usually hands you numbers you can't actually use. Here's how we really do it.

Step 1: we don't just paste a code

Most tutorials say: copy the gtag.js snippet, drop it in the <head>, done. We do it a little differently, because most of the sites we build also have a cookie banner — and the two need to talk to each other.

In practice: our Analytics tag starts out with "no consent" by default. Only once a visitor genuinely clicks "Accept" do we switch it on, through Google's Consent Mode. Sounds like a minor detail, but it matters a lot: without that link, you're measuring everyone, including people who explicitly said they didn't want that — not great, and shaky ground under GDPR.

Step 2: the first check is never the daily report

Once the tag is live, we don't look at the standard report — that's often hours behind. We open Realtime, click around the site ourselves, and check whether we show up as an active user. See nothing? Nine times out of ten you just haven't accepted the cookie banner yet in that browser session, or an ad blocker is stopping the request. Neither is a bug — just something to test for calmly before panicking.

The time we accidentally discovered bots

This is the part most Analytics explainers skip. Looking at a client's first month of data, we saw a spike of dozens of new visitors, all within a few hours on the same evening. Great, right? Until we checked the locations: at the top were places that aren't really "people" at all — well-known American data-center towns.

Turned out: we'd just sent pitch emails with a link to the site, and several recipients' email security (think Microsoft Defender, Proofpoint) had automatically opened that link to scan it — from a cloud server, not a laptop in someone's living room. Since then, before we trust a number like "143 visitors," we always check the hour-by-hour pattern and the locations first.

Pageviews are nice, leads are what matters

A page getting lots of views tells you nothing about whether anyone actually did something. That's why we always set up a separate "key event" for the moment someone truly submits a form — not when the page loads. That way you see, in black and white, how many visitors actually make an inquiry, instead of guessing based on a page counter that's partly made up of scanner traffic (see above).

Knowing which effort actually works

Sending a pitch email, posting on social, and running ads at the same time? Without extra effort, all of that disappears into the generic "(direct)" bucket — Analytics simply can't trace the source. So we always add a few extra characters to the link (UTM parameters) before it goes out. Takes ten seconds, and afterwards you know exactly which effort actually generated clicks — and clients.

Bottom line

Setting up Google Analytics takes five minutes. Setting it up properly — respecting cookie choices, keeping a sharp eye out for fake traffic, and focused on the numbers that actually matter — takes a bit more work, but saves you from a lot of wrong conclusions later.
